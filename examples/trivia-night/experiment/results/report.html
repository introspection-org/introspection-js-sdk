Introspection · Research report

Does remembering the crowd make a better quiz night?

A pre-registered test of agent memory in a simulated pub quiz, and of whether a memory policy written by the Introspection Operator beats having none.

Draft. Hypothesis 1 is confirmed on 20 held-out pubs, analysed as pre-registered (js-sdk tag trivia-night-prereg-v1, commit 200a9f72). Hypothesis 2 is not supported as pre-registered, because of a flaw in its measure; counted out of each night's 6 rounds, an exploratory analysis shows a large effect.

Abstract

We test whether a quiz-hosting agent that keeps notes on each pub runs a better second night there, and whether it is better because of what the notes say. Each pub's crowd is a seeded population of simulated people whose answers are decided by code, so the same team answers the same question the same way in every condition. A pub plays night 1, then night 2 four ways: with the host's own notes, with no notes, with another pub's notes as a placebo, and with the pub's true tastes as a ceiling. H1 was supported: on 20 held-out pubs, analysed exactly as pre-registered, the host's own notes raised the share of good rounds (rounds in a genre the crowd loves, pitched so 55–75% of answers are right) from 0.19 to 0.29 of a night's rounds (1.2 to 1.7 good rounds of 6), +0.10 (95% interval 0.04 to 0.16), and beat another pub's notes at 15 pubs to 1. That is 86% of the gap between no notes and the ceiling set by the pub's true tastes (descriptive). The gain came from remembering what the crowd likes (loved genres played 0.59 against 0.33), not from opening at a better level, which did not differ. The effect was about two thirds of its size on the development pubs. For H2, the Introspection Operator wrote a self-improved memory, starting from a recipe with no memory instructions. On the 20 held-out pubs H2 was not supported as pre-registered: the interval for its primary includes zero (+0.08 as a share of rounds played, 95% interval −0.08 to 0.23), because the pre-registered measure is a share of rounds played and three hosts without memory instructions stopped after one round, and that measure scores one good round then stopping as a perfect night. Counted out of each night's 6 rounds, an exploratory analysis chosen after seeing the data shows the self-improved memory roughly doubling good rounds (1.1 to 2.1 of 6, +1.0, 95% interval 0.5 to 1.5), mostly by playing loved genres (70% of rounds against 39%).

1. Introduction

Agents that serve the same customer repeatedly can keep notes between conversations. Whether that memory helps, and how to write the instructions that govern it, is usually judged by asking a model whether the answer with memory looks better. That is cheap and biased: language-model judges prefer answers that use memory more than people do [4]. We want a test where the outcome is measured, not judged.

A pub quiz is a good fit. A host who remembers a pub can do two concrete things better on a return visit: play the genres the crowd enjoys, and open at a difficulty the crowd can mostly answer. Both show in the crowd's answers. We build a simulated world where those answers come from code, give the host memory or not, and score the night from the answers alone.

We ask two questions. H1: does memory help, and is it the content of the memory that helps? H2: does a memory policy written by the Introspection Operator, starting from none, beat having none on pubs it never saw?

2. Related work

Self-improving memory. Chen et al. [1] optimise the instructions that tell an agent how to use memory, rewriting them from the agent's trajectories and scores. Li et al. [2] study an agent that revises a benchmark with feedback from the benchmark solvers and from external verifiers. PrisMem [3] "decomposes holistic memory evolution into capability-specific directions", noting that a single overall score can "hide capability-specific gains offset by regressions elsewhere"; our secondaries separate taste from level for the same reason.

Judging memory. Dong, He et al. [4] collected human preferences between responses with and without memory. A direct language-model judge and a reward model "select memory-conditioned responses in 67.2% and 81.1% of comparisons, respectively, compared with 45.0% for annotators."

What this study adds. None of the four includes a placebo-memory condition, so none can separate the effect of what was remembered from the effect of having memory at all. None reports paired intervals over independent units with sign tests and multiplicity correction; Chen et al. show 95% intervals, but on the best scores they observed, selected on the same task scores. Dong et al. show that judges over-credit memory, which is why every measure here is computed from the crowd's answers, and why our confirmation runs once, on pubs that played no part in selection.

3. The simulated world

Crowds and personas

Each pub is a seeded population. Per country (Australia, the UK, the US), a mix of age bands and eight interest archetypes is tilted by a scene drawn from the pub's id, then eight teams of three to six members are drawn from it; a team is friends of about one age, mostly of one leaning. All-star teams are rare by construction. On night 2, about four in five of night 1's teams return and new teams from the same mix fill the rest; every night-2 condition at a pub faces the same teams.

Answers are decided by code

No language model answers a question. A member knows an answer with probability

P(knows) = logistic(A + B·interest + C·cohort + D·(local or global ? 1 : −3) − E·difficulty)

where cohort is how much of the question's decade the member lived through between about 10 and 35. A team is right when any member knows it, times 0.9 for conferring. The roll is seeded by (seed, pub, question, team), so a team answers a question the same way on every night and in every condition; the only thing that differs between conditions is what the host chose to ask. Across the development runs, 85% of answers were right at difficulty 1, 69% at 2 and 52% at 3; at difficulty 2, a pub's favourite genres were answered right 75% of the time against 61% for its disliked genre; teams ranged from 45% to 87% right.

Tastes are read off the crowd, and cannot be guessed from the city

A pub's tastes are never set by hand: its favourites are the two genres its members are most interested in, its disliked genre the least, and its level the difficulty at which a crowd of five answers 65% of its favourites right. A host could otherwise guess tastes from a city's name, so each city has a written list of the two genres a host would guess, and a pub is redrawn while its favourites overlap that list.

The question bank

7,200 questions, 60 for each of 120 cells (six genres × four regions × five difficulties), half multiple choice and half short answer, written once by a model and then checked: a validation pass relabelled 1,146 genre and 740 region mismatches and 314 decades and removed 2,814 near-duplicate subjects. A pub never sees the same question twice; each question is assigned to one night by a hash.

Measures

  • Primary: good rounds. The share of a night's rounds that are both in a genre the crowd loves and 55–75% right; a night has 6 rounds, so 0.17 is one good round. Figures give it as good rounds out of 6; the statistics tables keep the share. It needs both things memory should get right at once.
  • Secondary: loved genres played (share of rounds in a loved genre: taste) and first round pitched right (night 2's opening round landed 55–75% right before any of the night's results could guide it: level).
  • Descriptive: closeness of the finish, lead changes and last place's share of the winner's points describe how the night felt and do not test memory.

Every measure is computed inside the trial's sandbox from the question bank's record of the crowd's answers, with no model involved.

Pre-registration, development and held-out pubs

The hypotheses, measures, analysis and decision rule were fixed before the confirmatory run, in a pre-registration frozen at js-sdk tag trivia-night-prereg-v1 (commit 200a9f72). 11 development pubs were used to build and check the world and the analysis; 20 held-out pubs, drawn with a fixed seed, were used once, for the confirmation. Analyses are paired by pub: 95% t-intervals on per-pub differences, exact two-sided sign tests with ties dropped, and Holm correction across the two secondary measures. Memory is supported when the model-minus-no-notes interval on good rounds excludes zero above and model notes beat other notes by sign test with p < 0.05.

Deviations and caveats

  1. One pub without a no-notes night. Newcastle's no-notes host played no rounds, so that night has no measures and the primary analysis uses 19 pubs. Counting it as zero good rounds would enlarge memory's lead, so leaving it out is the conservative choice.
  2. The placebo differed from its frozen definition. In this run the other-notes condition used the page's older planner: pubs could share one donor customer, and they loaded the donor's notes as rewritten after its night 2 rather than its night-1 notes, which is what the pre-registration defines. This was fixed after the run. The placebo still carried another pub's tastes, which is what it tests, but it was not exactly the frozen arm.
  3. The effect was smaller on new pubs. Held-out true notes minus no notes was +0.12 (95% interval 0.04 to 0.20), against +0.22 on development pubs, and model minus no notes was +0.10 against +0.15. The ≥ 0.15 world gate applies to development pubs only and changes no verdict here, but the effect shrinks on pubs nobody tuned against.
  4. One run, one host model, a simulation. Each condition ran once per pub with one host model, against simulated crowds, not people.

7. Discussion

Memory helped through taste, not pitch. With its own notes the host played a loved genre in 59% of rounds against 33% without notes, and true notes reached 67%; but the first round landed in the 55–75% band about as often with notes as without (0.60 against 0.47, not significant), and across the whole night rounds were pitched right slightly less often with notes (0.56 against 0.61). A host without notes reaches a reasonable level anyway by adjusting between rounds, which its instructions require, and a host steering towards loved genres sometimes overshoots their level before it has learned it. On a single return visit the content that pays is what the crowd likes.

The placebo shows the gain is in the content. Another pub's notes made the host confident about the wrong genres: other notes played loved genres in 27% of rounds, below no notes, and produced the fewest good rounds of any condition. Having notes, by itself, does not help.

A lesson for the next pre-registration. Score good rounds out of the night's fixed 6 rounds rather than the rounds played, and pre-specify a minimum-rounds rule for a night to count. H1 escaped this flaw only because its short night was a single, already excluded one; H2 did not.

The gain was real but smaller on held-out pubs. Nothing about the held-out pubs was tuned, so the smaller effect is the better estimate of what this host and memory policy do on a pub nobody has seen.

Why the primary missed: one-round nights

Under the original recipe the night-2 host stopped after a single round at Denver, Nottingham and Sheffield, and played none at Edinburgh. All 20 self-improved nights played 6 rounds. A share of rounds played scores one good round followed by stopping as 1.00, the best possible night: Denver and Nottingham each count as a perfect night with no memory instructions, against 2 of 6 with the self-improved memory. H1 is unaffected: its only short night was Newcastle's no-notes host, already excluded.

  • Austin. Its night 2 with no memory instructions failed three times before its host ran and succeeded on the fourth attempt: first a control-plane error (HTTP 500) while Docker was down, then two git fetch failures while the git server was down, each logged as "candidate Git fetch failed (exit status: 128)".   • Reruns. 20 nights were rerun; every one had failed before its host ran, and each was rerun as the same customer identity.

9. Limitations

  • A simulation. The crowds are illustrative, loosely shaped on adult age pyramids and what quiz-goers follow, not census-accurate. The effects are as large as the world makes them.
  • A deterministic crowd. Answers come from a formula, which makes conditions comparable but removes how real people respond to a host. A planned check replays the served questions to small language models playing the same personas.
  • Small samples. 11 development and 20 held-out pubs; first round pitched right is a single yes or no per pub and is under-powered.
  • Self-improved vs hand-written pairs two runs. It is pre-registered as a reported comparison, not a decision, and it pairs each pub's H1 and H2 runs, so the two nights were not played side by side.
  • Two nights. Whether notes stay useful over many weeks, as they grow and go stale, is untested.

10. Conclusion

A host that keeps its own notes on a pub runs a better second night there. On 20 held-out pubs, model notes beat no notes by about 0.6 good rounds of 6 and beat another pub's notes at 15 pubs to 1, so it is what the notes say that helps, not having notes at all. The notes recovered about 86% of what perfect notes give. Memory carried taste reliably: the host played the crowd's loved genres far more often. It did not carry level: the first round was not pitched right more often on either pub set.

The Introspection Operator, starting from no memory instructions, wrote a memory design that keeps taste apart from difficulty, labels how sure each belief is, and keeps a level per genre. On held-out pubs it raised the share of rounds in a loved genre from 39% to 70%. Its pre-registered test was not supported, because the measure scored hosts that stopped after one round as perfect; counted out of each night's 6 rounds, good rounds went from 1.1 to 2.1, a large gain that is exploratory and needs confirming.

Next: a pre-registered replication that scores good rounds out of 6 with a minimum-rounds rule; work on carrying level from one night to the next; and crowds simulated by language models, with other agents and domains.

Memory instructions, verbatim

Hand-written memory

recipe-operator/SYSTEM.md, between the memory markers, as shipped with the original trivia-night demo; identical in recipe commit 393c952, which the held-out H1 run used.

## Memory

The folder `memories/`, relative to where this conversation started, belongs to this venue. It is kept between this venue's nights and holds your notes about it in `memories/MEMORY.md`.

- **Before planning,** read `memories/MEMORY.md` if it exists. Use what it says to shape tonight's plan, and say how in the plan. If there are no notes, this is your first night here: ask which country the venue is in, then start at difficulty 3 with a balanced mix of genres.
- **The bank holds the facts:** what was asked, and how this venue did by genre and by difficulty (`round_summary`). Don't copy them into the notes.
- **The notes hold your judgement:** the venue's country, the genres this crowd enjoys and the ones it doesn't, and the difficulty to start the next night at, each with a few words of why.
- **Never write in the notes** team names, scores, standings, or any question or answer that was asked.
- **A request for tonight is never a lasting preference.** Don't write it into the notes unless the trivia master says it should always be that way here.
- **After the last round,** rewrite `memories/MEMORY.md` (replace it, don't append) so it's ready for next week.

## Memory

`memories/` is kept between nights at this pub, and `memories/MEMORY.md` is loaded at the start of every night.

- **Before you start:** read `memories/profile.md` if MEMORY.md points to it. Follow every line marked `confirmed`; lean towards lines marked `guess`. Then say in one sentence how the profile shapes tonight's plan: the loved genre's share of rounds and, for each genre you will play, the level you open it at.
- profile.md holds two kinds of belief, kept apart:
  - **Taste:** what the pub says its crowd likes or wants every week. What the pub said is `confirmed` and stays so until the pub itself says otherwise. How rounds scored never weakens it: a crowd that struggles in a genre it loves needs a lower level there, not fewer rounds of it.
  - **Fit:** one line per genre: which levels ran easy, landed near the target or ran hard for this crowd, and the level to open that genre at next night. It is a `guess` after one night, `confirmed` when another night agrees, and removed when a later night contradicts it. A genre never played has no fit line.
- **Before you finish:** rewrite profile.md, one line per belief: the belief, `taste` or `fit`, `confirmed` or `guess`, and the evidence in a few words (the genre and level, never a team). Tonight's rounds change only their own genre's line; a genre not played tonight keeps its line as it was.
- Never written as a belief: team names, scores, standings, any question or answer, or a request for tonight only (how many rounds, their size, how answers come back). A one-night request stays one even when it is asked again on a later night.
- Keep MEMORY.md an index, one line: `- This pub: profile.md`.

Reproducibility

  • Each night is an evaluation trial on the Introspection platform: the host runs at a fixed recipe commit, memory is kept per simulated customer, and the question bank runs beside it as a sidecar that the trial's own verifier reads.
  • The 66 development trials cost $5.67 at list price, $0.086 a trial.
  • The world, the question bank and its generator, the task generator, the pre-registration and the analysis are in the open-source trivia-night example in the Introspection JavaScript SDK; the pre-registration is frozen at tag trivia-night-prereg-v1.
